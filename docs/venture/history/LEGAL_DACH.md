# Legal and regulatory assessment: online physiotherapy in Germany, Austria and Switzerland

**Prepared:** 12 September 2026
**Scope:** private-pay and privately insured customers, remote video appointments, digital exercise guidance and packages
**Status:** counsel-ready first pass, not legal advice

This memo separates verified law from operating inference and questions for local counsel. It assumes the service is delivered by qualified physiotherapists, that the company sells care directly to consumers, and that the company may use a shared digital platform. The answer changes materially if the company employs clinicians in one country while treating patients in another, uses non-physiotherapists, makes diagnostic claims through software, or treats minors.

## Decision

Do not launch one undifferentiated DACH service from a single entity and assume that a video call is lawful everywhere. Launch a narrow, clinician-led private-pay service with country-specific clinical responsibility, local professional checks, a documented triage gate, and country-specific terms and privacy notices.

The lowest legal friction is usually:

1. Start in one country where the treating clinicians are properly authorized and insured.
2. Use video for assessment, education, exercise prescription and follow-up only where the clinician can meet the applicable professional standard.
3. Exclude cases needing hands-on examination, manipulation, urgent assessment, imaging or post-operative review without surgical-team coordination.
4. Add the second and third country only after a local health-law review, professional authorization check and insurance confirmation.

**Stop sign:** “fully online” cannot mean “no local clinical presence, no physical referral path and no examination boundary.” In Germany in particular, a physiotherapist who independently determines and treats illness without the required authorization may trigger the Heilpraktikergesetz even if the customer pays privately. The protected professional title under the MPhG is a separate issue from permission to practise healing arts.

## Germany

### Professional authorization and direct access

**Verified law.** MPhG §1 protects the title “Physiotherapeut/in” and requires permission to use it. The statute also provides a limited route for EEA professionals providing a temporary and occasional service, subject to notification and verification. This is not a general permission to treat German residents from anywhere.

**Verified law.** Heilpraktikergesetz §1 defines healing practice broadly as professional activity to determine, heal or alleviate illness, suffering or bodily injury, and requires permission for anyone doing so without medical licensure. A German physiotherapist may have a professional qualification yet still need a relevant Heilpraktiker authorization or another legal basis for independent diagnosis and treatment without a physician’s prescription. The exact interaction is fact-sensitive and must be confirmed with the competent state authority and German health lawyer.

**Operating inference.** A German launch should either use clinicians whose authorization expressly covers the intended direct-access activity, or structure the first product as education, supervised exercise and prevention with a formal clinical triage and referral path. Calling the service “physiotherapy treatment” while avoiding the authorization analysis is unsafe.

### Remote care and advertising

**Verified law.** HWG §9 restricts advertising for remote diagnosis or treatment that is not based on personal perception of the person being treated. It contains an exception where a personal medical contact is not required under generally accepted professional standards. HWG §3 separately prohibits misleading therapeutic efficacy claims and implying guaranteed success.

**Operating inference.** A live video assessment with a qualified clinician is stronger than an automated questionnaire, but it does not make every condition suitable for remote care. Landing pages should describe the exact remote service, the clinician’s qualification and limitations. Avoid “diagnosis in minutes,” “guaranteed recovery,” before-and-after claims, unsupported cure rates and claims that a physical visit is unnecessary.

### Records, privacy and consumer flow

Use a German-compliant Impressum under the DDG, a German privacy notice and a documented record of processing. Health data is GDPR special-category data. Core care should be mapped to a contract necessity basis under Art. 6 with a valid Art. 9 condition, commonly healthcare-related processing with professional secrecy and, where needed, explicit consent for optional uses. Marketing analytics, retargeting and testimonials need separate analysis.

Use a German-hosted or EU-region video and clinical-record provider where possible. Sign DPAs, list subprocessors, restrict staff access, encrypt in transit and at rest, and define deletion and legal-retention exceptions. A data-protection impact assessment is likely prudent because the service processes health data at scale and may use systematic online intake.

## Austria

### Professional and telemedicine rules

**Verified law.** MTDG 2024 §28(3) states that advice or treatment through information and communication technologies may be provided where professionally defensible and lege artis. Patients must be informed about the particularities of treatment through communication media. The same statute requires professional duties, cooperation and referral when medical clarification is needed.

**Verified law.** MTDG §34 requires documentation of measures, inspection on request and a free first copy. Freelance records and documentation are retained for ten years. §36 imposes confidentiality. §39 contains advertising, information and invoicing rules, and §40 requires professional liability insurance.

**Operating inference.** Austria is the clearest legal candidate for a properly qualified, clinician-led remote pilot. The telemedicine permission is conditional. It does not authorize a clinician to deliver every treatment remotely, bypass referral, or treat outside the professional scope. The patient’s location, the clinician’s Berufssitz, registration and temporary cross-border service route still need checking.

### Cross-border services

**Verified law.** MTDG §50 allows recognized EEA or Swiss professionals legally established in another EEA state or Switzerland to provide temporary services in Austria under the statutory conditions. It requires evidence of lawful practice and no suspension. This route is not a substitute for an establishment analysis for a permanent platform, repeated local activity or an Austrian-employed team.

**Counsel questions.** Confirm whether the proposed model is temporary service provision or Austrian establishment, which registration and notification steps apply, whether the company can contract with clinicians as contractors, and how professional liability and record custody work after the clinician leaves.

## Switzerland

### Authorization and cantonal supervision

**Verified law.** The Federal Office of Public Health states that physiotherapists are among the GesBG professions and need a Berufsausübungsbewilligung when working in their own professional responsibility. The permit is issued by the canton where the profession is practised. The requirement can also reach an employed lead or the only physiotherapist in a facility without same-profession supervision.

**Operating inference.** A Swiss customer’s residence should be treated as a Swiss regulatory trigger until the target canton confirms the opposite. There is no safe assumption that a German or Austrian platform can deliver Swiss clinical care without a Swiss permit or recognized arrangement. Start with one named canton and obtain written authority guidance before serving residents there.

### Reimbursement and self-pay

**Verified fact.** BAG describes the national physiotherapy tariff for ambulatory services. The compulsory-insurance pathway is not the same as a private-pay package. BAG materials distinguish physiotherapy from professions that may be reimbursed without a physician’s prescription. Any marketing that suggests basic insurance will pay for a package needs an insurer-specific and tariff-specific basis.

**Operating inference.** Switzerland may support attractive private-pay economics, but the price must cover Swiss clinical, payment, insurance and compliance costs. Quote prices in CHF, state reimbursement uncertainty plainly and provide invoices that let customers submit claims themselves when appropriate.

## Health data, consent, records and platform design

The platform should be designed as a clinical service first and a booking funnel second.

| Control | Minimum operating requirement |
|---|---|
| Data map | List symptoms, diagnosis, videos, exercise plans, messages, payment data, device data, marketing identifiers, recipients, region and retention. |
| Roles | The provider is usually controller for patient care. A scheduling, video, payment or record vendor is usually processor, subject to a written Art. 28 DPA where GDPR applies. |
| Intake | Collect only what supports triage and care. Do not ask for broad medical histories “just in case.” |
| Consent | Obtain informed consent for remote-care limits, recording if any, optional communications and data uses. Consent cannot repair an unsafe clinical model. |
| Recording | Do not record sessions by default. If recording is necessary, document purpose, retention, access and a separate explicit consent flow. |
| Access | Provide patient access and export. Austria expressly requires inspection and a free first copy; GDPR rights remain relevant in Germany, Austria and for EU residents. |
| Retention | Set country-aware clinical and tax retention schedules. Austria’s MTDG baseline is ten years for freelance records. Do not promise deletion where a legal retention duty applies. |
| Transfers | Prefer EU/EEA processing. For Swiss processing or Swiss providers, map GDPR Chapter V and revDSG transfer rules. Obtain counsel review for US vendors and government-access risk. |
| Security | MFA, least privilege, audit logs, secure link expiry, clinician device policy, breach response, backups and tested restore. |

## Clinical boundary by segment

The clinical scope is a legal and safety control, not only a marketing choice.

| Segment | Remote suitability | Launch rule |
|---|---|---|
| Sports performance, load management, return-to-training education | Often suitable for video plus self-reported measures when no red flags exist. | Good initial wedge. Do not promise clearance for competition or return from a serious injury without required in-person assessment. |
| Non-specific musculoskeletal pain | Sometimes suitable after red-flag screening and functional assessment. | Accept only low-risk presentations with clear escalation and local referral options. |
| Post-operative rehabilitation | Mixed. Protocol and surgeon coordination matter. | Require documented surgical/medical plan and a physical-care fallback. Exclude complications and wound concerns. |
| Pelvic health | Mixed and sensitive. Some care can be education and exercise, but examination and safeguarding may be needed. | Counsel review, specialist clinicians, privacy safeguards and strict referral criteria before launch. |
| Neurological, cardiopulmonary, acute trauma, severe pain or unexplained symptoms | Often unsuitable for fully remote first contact. | Exclude from automated purchase. Direct to urgent or in-person care. |
| Minors, pregnancy-related concerns, suspected abuse or safeguarding issues | Higher consent and safeguarding burden. | Exclude in phase one unless specialist governance exists. |

These are clinical operating inferences, not legal classifications. Have the medical director approve an inclusion/exclusion protocol, red-flag list and escalation script for each country.

## Packages, refunds and insurance language

Sell a package as a clearly described service bundle, with the number and duration of live sessions, messaging scope, exercise-plan updates, expiry, rescheduling, no-show treatment, clinician substitution, refunds and clinical termination rights. Separate live care from downloadable digital content and generic education because consumer withdrawal treatment may differ.

EU distance-contract rules generally provide a 14-day withdrawal right, subject to exceptions. The healthcare and social-services exclusion and the digital-service rules can produce different outcomes for a mixed package. If performance starts during the withdrawal period, obtain the legally required express request and acknowledgement, and calculate any lawful pro-rata amount. Have counsel draft country-specific checkout language and a compliant withdrawal function where required.

Never make private insurance reimbursement a core promise. Use: “You may be able to submit the invoice to your insurer. Coverage depends on your plan and the insurer’s decision.” Ask each insurer or customer for its current reimbursement requirements before issuing a coverage claim.

## Required counsel work before launch

Obtain written advice from a German medical-regulatory lawyer, an Austrian health-law lawyer and a Swiss lawyer familiar with the target canton. Ask them to answer:

1. Does the proposed clinician qualification and model permit direct-access diagnosis and treatment in each country, especially Germany?
2. Where is professional practice deemed to occur for a patient in country A treated by a clinician in country B?
3. Is an entity-level health-facility, practice, notification or registration requirement triggered?
4. What exact remote-care documentation, informed-consent and referral standard applies to the launch conditions?
5. Which professional liability policies cover remote care, cross-border care and contractors?
6. Which claims, testimonials, influencer partnerships and paid-search language are lawful under local advertising rules?
7. Which package, cancellation, refund and withdrawal terms apply to live care, asynchronous messaging and digital exercise content?
8. What clinical-record retention, access and handover obligations apply if the platform closes or a clinician leaves?
9. Does the software remain a wellness tool, or does triage, diagnosis, treatment recommendation or monitoring create a medical-device qualification issue under the EU MDR or Swiss rules?
10. What entity, VAT, permanent-establishment and invoicing treatment applies to cross-border consumer services? Use a tax adviser for the answer.

## Practical launch controls

Before accepting payment:

- verify every clinician’s title, recognition, registration and location;
- obtain certificates of professional liability covering telehealth and target countries;
- publish the legal entity, responsible clinicians, prices, cancellation rules, privacy notice and emergency instructions;
- run a clinician-led video intake with identity/location confirmation and red-flag screening;
- create “stop and refer” rules for emergencies, neurological deficits, major trauma, unexplained systemic symptoms, post-operative complications and cases needing examination;
- use a compliant EU/Swiss-capable video and records stack with signed processor agreements;
- test a patient access request, record export, deletion request and incident response before launch;
- have counsel approve the checkout, consent, package and advertising copy in each country.

## Confidence and limits

High confidence: the cited statutory text and regulator explanations.
Medium confidence: the operational interpretation of where cross-border online practice occurs, because facts, clinician status and canton/state practice change the analysis.
Low confidence until counsel confirms: direct-access treatment in Germany, Swiss canton treatment of remote-only providers, contractor/entity structure, consumer-law treatment of mixed packages, VAT and any medical-device classification.

This is a research memo, not a legal opinion. It should be handed to local counsel with the actual entity, clinician locations, service flow, patient locations, software vendors and launch copy attached.
