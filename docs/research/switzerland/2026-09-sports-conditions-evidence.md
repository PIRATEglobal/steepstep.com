# Sports conditions for Steepstep

**Research date:** 12 September 2026
**Market:** Switzerland, Swiss German, private-pay online physiotherapy
**Question:** Which sports and activity-related problems deserve dedicated Steepstep pages, what does good first-line rehabilitation look like, and which parts can be delivered safely by video?

## Executive recommendation

Build a small, high-quality sports cluster around the problems people actually search for and recognise:

1. Anterior or patellofemoral knee pain, especially for runners, hikers, skiers and strength trainees.
2. Lateral ankle sprain and recurrent ankle instability.
3. Achilles tendon pain and calf loading problems.
4. Hamstring or other lower-limb muscle strain, with a clear return-to-running path.
5. Non-specific low-back pain in active adults, including the question of returning to training.

Treat these as symptom-led education and care pages, not diagnosis claims. A visitor should quickly learn whether a video assessment is a reasonable next step, what the rehabilitation process would involve, and when a doctor, imaging, urgent assessment or in-person examination is needed.

Do not publish thin pages for every body part. Add shoulder pain, plantar heel pain, hip pain, cycling-related pain or skiing-specific ACL recovery only when Steepstep has a named expert, a real protocol, and enough first-hand experience to explain assessment, progression and referral. This keeps topical depth credible and avoids a programmatic catalogue with little value.

## Condition decisions

### 1. Anterior knee pain / patellofemoral pain

**Why it earns a page.** It is common in runners and active adults, the name people use is often simply “knieschmerzen”, and it maps well to a structured interview, movement observation, strength work and progressive loading. It also connects naturally to running, hiking, skiing and gym training.

**What good care addresses.** Start with the person’s training history, symptom behaviour, goals and relevant impairments. The best-practice synthesis places education and knee-targeted with or without hip-targeted exercise at the centre. Foot orthoses, taping, manual therapy or running retraining may be supporting options for a suitable presentation, not a fixed package. Avoid presenting “tracking” or one faulty movement as the universal cause.

**What video can do.** A clinician can take a history, observe a squat, step-down, lunge or sport-specific movement within a safe home setup, review training load, check exercise technique, and progress strength and tolerance over time. Video cannot reliably establish every differential diagnosis or replace hands-on assessment where swelling, locking, instability, acute trauma or a surgical question is present.

**Page promise.** “Knieschmerzen beim Laufen, Wandern oder Sport: herausfinden, was dein Knie gerade belastet, und Schritt für Schritt wieder belastbarer werden.” The page must say that “patellofemoral pain” is a clinical description, not a conclusion from a web form.

**Safety route.** Acute major trauma, inability to bear weight, a deformed knee, a hot/red joint with fever, a locked knee, rapidly increasing swelling or repeated giving-way should trigger medical assessment. A remote clinician should refer when history and movement screen do not make a safe conservative path plausible.

### 2. Lateral ankle sprain and recurrent instability

**Why it earns a page.** Ankle sprains are recognisable, high-intent and relevant to football, trail running, hiking, skiing and court sports. They also have a clear progression from protection and movement to strength, balance and sport-specific confidence.

**What good care addresses.** The ankle CPG recommends a structured therapeutic exercise programme selected for injury severity, impairments, preferences and learning needs. That can include protected active range of motion, stretching, neuromuscular training, postural re-education and balance work. Return to impact should be progressive, with strength, range, balance and sport movements tested rather than chosen by a calendar alone. The NHS patient guidance notes that many ankle sprains take 8 to 12 weeks, and severe or high ankle sprains can take longer. This is a useful expectation-setting range, not a Steepstep promise.

**What video can do.** History, injury mechanism, swelling and weight-bearing questions, safe visual movement checks, calf raises, balance progressions and home exercise coaching are suitable after a screen. A clinician can help decide whether the person needs urgent or in-person assessment. Video cannot rule out a fracture or syndesmotic injury as confidently as an examination and imaging pathway when Ottawa-style concerns, marked bony tenderness or inability to walk are present.

**Page promise.** “Umgeknickt? Wir klären, was du jetzt belasten kannst, bauen Stabilität auf und planen die Rückkehr zu deinem Sport.” Use “kann passen” and “muss abgeklärt werden”, not “wir behandeln jeden Bänderriss online”.

**Safety route.** Same-day assessment is indicated for severe pain, substantial or worsening swelling/bruising, inability to take more than a few steps, deformity, numbness/tingling, a cold or blue foot, a crack or suspected fracture, or fever. Persistent swelling, pain or giving-way warrants in-person review.

### 3. Achilles tendon pain

**Why it earns a page.** It is a high-intent problem among runners and active adults, frequently linked to sudden changes in running, hill work, jumping or training volume. It suits Steepstep’s load-management and sports background, but requires honest time horizons.

**What good care addresses.** Explain the difference between symptoms around the tendon and a confirmed diagnosis. Build a load history, examine how walking, calf raises, running, hills and jumping affect symptoms, then use a progressive calf and lower-limb strengthening plan. The guidelines support gradual training build-up and tailored exercise. Tendons often improve over months, so avoid quick-fix language, fixed “healing” timelines or claims that an exercise can repair a tendon overnight.

**What video can do.** It can map symptom irritability, observe gait and calf loading, coach progressive exercise, adjust running and jumping exposure and monitor next-day response. It cannot safely assess a suspected acute rupture or every insertional, bursae, nerve or referred-pain differential without an in-person pathway.

**Page promise.** “Achillessehne gereizt? Wir bringen Belastung, Kraft und Sport wieder in ein Verhältnis, das dein Körper verträgt.” Explain that the target is improved capacity and function, not chasing zero sensation after every session.

**Safety route.** Sudden snap or pop, immediate loss of push-off, a palpable gap, major bruising or inability to walk requires urgent in-person assessment for rupture. Increasing redness, warmth, fever, marked swelling or unexplained calf swelling requires medical review.

### 4. Hamstring or lower-limb muscle strain

**Why it earns a page.** Hamstring injury is particularly relevant to sprinting, football and other running or pivoting sports. The 2023 international consensus describes hamstring injuries as the most common athletic injury in running and pivoting sports, while also noting that injury rates have not declined over two decades.

**What good care addresses.** Rehabilitation should be individualised to the athlete, sport demand, involved muscle and injury severity. Early stages avoid high-strain loads. Progress through strength and lengthening work, kinetic-chain capacity, running and then sprinting. Progression should use symptoms and capacity, and the end point should reflect the demands of the sport. There is no single validated calendar or universal strength benchmark, so Steepstep should not sell a guaranteed “return in X days”.

**What video can do.** It can identify mechanism and location, guide a graded home strength programme, observe low-risk movements, review running clips and plan a gradual return. A suspected complete tear, avulsion, major bruising, severe loss of function or recurrent injury needs an in-person examination and possibly imaging.

**Page promise.** “Oberschenkel gezerrt oder beim Sprint eingeschossen? Wir führen dich von kontrollierter Belastung zurück zu Tempo und Sport.” The copy should distinguish a mild strain pattern from a diagnosis that needs examination.

**Safety route.** Urgent evaluation for severe pain, deformity, rapidly expanding bruising/swelling, inability to walk or a pop with major weakness. Persistent focal pain at the sitting bone, recurrent strains or failure to progress require clinical review.

### 5. Non-specific low-back pain in active adults

**Why it earns a page.** Low-back pain has the broadest demand. WHO reports 619 million people experienced it in 2020 and calls it the leading cause of disability worldwide. For Steepstep, the useful angle is active adults who want to keep moving, lift, run, hike or return to training.

**What good care addresses.** Screen for specific causes and neurological concerns first. For uncomplicated presentations, combine clear education, staying active, pacing and a tailored exercise programme. WHO recommends person-centred, coordinated care for chronic primary low-back pain and lists exercise among recommended interventions. The JOSPT guideline supports trunk strength/endurance, movement control and general exercise according to presentation. Do not make posture correction, spinal “realignment”, traction or a single passive technique the core promise.

**What video can do.** It is well suited to a detailed history, function and goal assessment, movement observation, education, graded exercise, work and training advice, and follow-up of response. It can support a person who has already been medically assessed. It cannot replace neurological examination or urgent assessment when red flags are present.

**Page promise.** “Rückenschmerzen und trotzdem aktiv bleiben: Wir ordnen deine Belastung, geben dir einen klaren Plan und begleiten den Aufbau.” Avoid implying that every back pain episode has a discoverable structural cause.

**Safety route.** Escalate urgently for new bladder or bowel dysfunction, saddle numbness, progressive or severe neurological weakness, major trauma, unexplained fever, cancer history, significant systemic illness or other clinician-identified red flags. NICE specifically says clinicians should exclude cancer, infection, trauma and inflammatory disease and use risk-informed management.

## Cross-condition online care model

Every condition page should explain the same clinical path in plain Swiss German:

1. **Erstgespräch, 20 Minuten kostenlos:** goals, history, current load, symptom behaviour, prior diagnosis and a safety screen. This is orientation, not a diagnosis promise or emergency service.
2. **Video assessment:** simple, safe movements in a prepared space; no forced tests and no painful performance for the camera.
3. **Plan:** a small number of exercises, a load rule, a way to track response, and a clear next checkpoint.
4. **Progression:** adjust frequency, range, resistance, speed and sport exposure from observed capacity and next-day response.
5. **Return to activity:** test the movements and demands that matter to the person’s sport, with shared decisions about readiness and remaining risk.
6. **Referral:** if the presentation falls outside safe remote scope, Steepstep should help the person find local medical or hands-on care rather than stretch the service beyond its competence.

The 2024 APTA telehealth guideline and 2024 systematic review support video care as a serious delivery mode when prepared and implemented well. The review found comparable satisfaction and, in the included trials, a small attendance and adherence advantage. The site should present this as evidence that video can work for selected MSK rehabilitation, never as proof that online care is equivalent for every injury.

## What should remain out of the online promise

Do not position the site as an emergency service, imaging service, surgical decision service, hands-on manual treatment service, or substitute for local care after major trauma. Do not accept symptom forms that invite detailed health histories before a secure clinical process exists. The contact form should explicitly ask people not to send medical or diagnosis information by email.

For each condition, the CTA should offer a free 20-minute orientation call and state the boundary: “Ob das online für dich passt, klären wir gemeinsam. Bei Warnzeichen verweisen wir dich an die passende medizinische Stelle.”

### Steepstep’s editorial stance

The synthesis above supports five claims Steepstep can own:

- We start with the person’s sport, goals, symptom behaviour and current capacity, not a label selected from a menu.
- We use movement and load as information. A movement observation helps guide a plan; it is not a remote substitute for every clinical test.
- We make the next step small enough to do, then make it more demanding as capacity grows.
- We define progress by useful function, confidence and sport demand, with symptoms interpreted in context.
- We say clearly when video is a good fit and when a local examination, imaging or urgent medical care is the right next move.

These are interpretations from the cited guidelines and reviews, not direct quotations or claims that Steepstep has measured outcomes. A named practitioner must approve the final wording and exercise examples.

## SEO and GEO implications

Use the terms people actually use alongside clinical terms: “Knieschmerzen beim Laufen”, “umgeknickt Knöchel”, “Achillessehne Schmerzen”, “Oberschenkel gezerrt”, “Rückenschmerzen beim Sport”, “Physio online Schweiz” and “Sportphysio online”. Swiss German copy can retain standard German clinical vocabulary where that is what searchers understand; do not force dialect spellings that reduce clarity.

Each page should answer one main question in the first 80 to 120 words, then show the assessment and care path. Use concise tables for “online passend / ärztlich abklären”, visible source links, FAQ sections that answer real questions, `Article` or `MedicalWebPage` schema only if the visible page supports it, and a named `Person` author linked to the expert profile. No medical outcome statistics should be used without a source and population context.

Do not create near-duplicate pages for “Knieschmerzen”, “Knie Schmerzen”, “Läuferknie” and “Runner’s knee” unless the intent and content truly differ. Build one strong hub and use sport pages for distinct activity intent.

## Open evidence gaps

- No public Swiss search-volume or Search Console data was available in this research pass. Priority is based on clinical commonness, user-recognisable intent, fit with the stated sports expertise and remote-care suitability, not measured Steepstep demand.
- Swiss legal and professional rules for the exact provider entity, cross-canton service delivery and prescribing/referral arrangements need a licensed Swiss review before public claims are final.
- The evidence supports selected MSK telerehabilitation, but no source establishes that Steepstep’s exact video model, staff mix or outcomes will match published trials.
- Clinical content must be reviewed by the actual licensed practitioners named on the site before publication. The research is not medical advice and must not be presented as a diagnostic protocol for the public.

## Source handling rule for the build

Paraphrase evidence in original Swiss German. Link to the source rather than copying guideline text. Keep a page-level source register with title, publisher, publication/update date, URL, access date and the exact claim supported. Recheck current versions before launch because clinical guidelines and Swiss requirements can change.

### Scannability pattern to adopt

The best references use the same order because it matches a worried person’s decisions:

1. **Could this be relevant to me?** A two-sentence symptom pattern, with a reminder that it is not a diagnosis.
2. **Do I need help today?** A short, high-contrast red-flag panel with the appropriate Swiss route to medical care.
3. **Is online care a fit?** A clear list of what the clinician can observe and what requires hands-on or medical assessment.
4. **What happens next?** The Steepstep assessment, plan, progression and check-in path.
5. **How long might this take?** Source-linked ranges or honest “varies by severity and response”, never a promise.
6. **How do I return to my sport?** Capacity and task markers, not a calendar alone.
7. **Who wrote and reviewed this?** Named practitioner, qualifications, review date and source list.

Use an anchored local navigation on desktop and a compact sticky section control on mobile. Use exercise cards only where the practitioner has supplied the exact cueing, dosage, contraindications and progression. Static diagrams or short self-hosted videos can help, but each asset must have alt text, captions where needed, and a warning that a public page is not an individual prescription.

### Duration corrections for final copy

- Mild hamstring injuries can improve in days, while severe injuries may take weeks or months and can require specialist treatment. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hamstring-injury/)
- Patellofemoral pain resources commonly state that recovery can take 12 weeks or more. [University Hospitals Liverpool](https://www.uhliverpool.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-leaflets/patellofemoral-pain)
- Ankle sprains often take 8 to 12 weeks, with severe and high ankle sprains taking longer. [North Bristol NHS Trust](https://www.nbt.nhs.uk/our-services/a-z-services/emergency-zone/ed-miu-patient-information/ankle-injuries)
- Achilles rehabilitation commonly takes months; Guy’s and St Thomas’ says many people need 6 to 9 months before full activities without pain. [GSTT](https://www.guysandstthomas.nhs.uk/health-information/achilles-tendinopathy)
- Low-back pain duration should be described by acute, subacute and chronic categories and by function, not by a fixed “healing” date. NICE defines chronic as 3 months or longer and WHO’s chronic primary LBP guideline is explicitly for persistent presentations. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations), [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/b/71563)
